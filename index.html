01

The trouble with a one-line change

Nothing about a small change is small. The copy on the pricing page is wrong by one word, and fixing it costs a ticket, a branch, a local environment that has drifted since the last time you opened it, and a context switch out of whatever you were actually doing.

So the word stays wrong. Not because anyone decided to keep it, but because the cost of the fix is paid in the one currency that is always short. Teams accumulate a quiet backlog of things everybody agrees about and nobody does: the stale link, the heading that says the old product name, the button that should be one shade darker. Work that takes ninety seconds once you are in the file, and half an afternoon to get into the file.

The usual answer is to make the ceremony faster — better scripts, a warmer cache, a codespace that boots in twenty seconds instead of four minutes. That helps, and it does not change the shape of the problem. The change still has to be carried, by a person, from the sentence in their head to the diff in the repository.

A change you can describe out loud in one breath should not need a branch, a checkout and an afternoon.

02

What happens when you just type it

You write the change the way you would say it to the person sitting next to you, and Prompt Bar does the carrying.

› add a pricing page with three tiers

  1. It knows which project you mean

    Prompt Bar reads the services, repositories and domains you already have, so “the footer” resolves to the footer of the site that serves the domain you were looking at — not a file with a similar name in a repository nobody has deployed since spring. When two readings are genuinely possible, it asks one question instead of guessing twice.

  2. It works on a live preview, not your laptop

    Every change gets its own machine with the project running on it and a URL you can open. You read the new pricing page in a browser, on a phone, with a colleague looking over your shoulder, before a single line is proposed to the default branch. Nothing is installed, nothing drifts, nothing has to be torn down afterwards.

  3. It ends in a pull request, never a surprise

    The work lands the way work has always landed here: a draft pull request on your repository, co-authored, with a real commit message and a diff a reviewer can argue with. Your checks run. Your review rules hold. Merging is still a decision a person makes, and it is still the moment the change goes live.

Three things, in order. Find the right service. Build it on a preview. Open the pull request.

The preview is the part people underestimate. Reading the change is faster than reasoning about it.

03

What you get back

Time, mostly — but the useful part is what the time is made of.

The small things get done on the day somebody notices them, which means they stop being a backlog at all. The people who see the problem first — support, design, the person writing the release notes — can describe the fix themselves and hand a reviewable diff to an engineer, rather than handing over a description and waiting for a translation. And the engineer keeps the afternoon they were going to spend re-learning a repository they wrote two years ago.

What does not change is the part you would not want changed. The review still happens. The tests still run. Nothing reaches production because a sentence was persuasive.

Ninety seconds of work should cost about ninety seconds.
